Autonomous UAS & Rover Network

A two-semester senior design project at CU Boulder. We are designing a cooperative autonomous UAS Multirotor and Ground Rover. The multirotor autonomously locates a target payload site and relays the coordinates to the rover, which drives to it and delivers a payload. I'm the Dynamics & Controls Lead on the Rover.

01

Overview

In search and exploration missions it can be advantageous to use a multi-agent network of air and land-based vehicles to cover an area and deliver or retrieve payloads at locations with reduced accessibility, where the precise location may not initially be known.

Each vehicle type has an advantage in its own environment: the UAS can maneuver over terrain to search a wide area but may not be able to land where clearance is limited, while the rover can operate on terrain the UAS cannot reach. In this project a UAS multirotor performs the search, exploration, and precise localization of a point of interest (for example, a stranded hiker), and a rover handles payload delivery or retrieval once that location is known. The two vehicles work autonomously and cooperatively to increase the overall chance of mission success.

02

Mission & concept of operations

The reference mission profile the network is built around:

  1. Takeoff. The UAS is given the coordinates of an area of interest and takes off from starting point A (manual control).
  2. Transit. The UAS flies to the area of interest (autonomous / semi-autonomous with manual override).
  3. Search. The UAS searches within the area of interest to detect an ArUco marker.
  4. Relay. The UAS detects the marker and relays its coordinates to the rover or base station.
  5. Return. The UAS returns to its starting location.
  6. Rover transit. The rover receives the marker coordinates and moves from starting point B toward the marker.
  7. Payload. The rover delivers or retrieves a payload within a radius of the marker (manual control).
  8. Rover return. The rover returns to its starting location.

05

Dynamics & Controls plan

An outline of the Dynamics & Controls work:

  • Equations of motion: derive the rover's EOM for X–Y planar movement and the payload-delivery mechanism.
  • Dynamics simulation: model and visualize the rover's motion in MATLAB/Python from defined design requirements.
  • Control laws: trade studies of candidate control laws for precise, repeatable payload delivery.
  • Autonomy & override: autonomous rover motion with a manual override available on every phase.
06

Trade studies

Key design decisions were made through formal trade studies, scoring each option against weighted criteria:

  • Control architecture: PID vs. LQT vs. pure-pursuit, scored on path-tracking accuracy, computational cost, obstacle avoidance, and tuning complexity. LQT was selected (weighted score 4.2, ahead of PID at 4.1 and pursuit at 3.6).
  • Steering mechanism: skid-steer vs. Ackerman, weighing modeling complexity, maneuverability, steering radius, and energy efficiency.
  • Number of wheels: 2 vs. 4 vs. 6+ wheels, trading terrain mobility, traction, weight, and obstacle clearance — more wheels lowered peak per-wheel torque (from 2.5 N·m at 4 wheels to 1.25 N·m at 8).
  • Inertial reference point: where the shared coordinate frame is anchored (base station vs. drone start), weighing reference loss, GPS dependence, and error accumulation.

The selected LQT controller reached millimeter-level mean cross-track error over a 1,000-path Monte Carlo (nominal model, 0.5 m/s), and its gains follow automatically from the Q and R weights rather than manual tuning.

08

Timeline & milestones

The project runs across two semesters against a schedule of formal design reviews, each with a hard deadline. The design has to be presented, defended, and signed off before the team moves to the next phase:

  1. Conceptual design (CONOPS): lock in the concept of operations, requirements, and candidate architectures for the UAS and rover.
  2. Preliminary Design Review (PDR): present the baselined architecture and subsystem approach, with early analysis showing the design is feasible.
  3. Critical Design Review (CDR): a detailed, build-ready design: subsystem designs finalized, interfaces frozen, and analysis backing every requirement.
  4. Prototyping & manufacturing: build and integrate the UAS (and rover) subsystems and bring them together into a working vehicle.
  5. Test Readiness Review (TRR): test plans, procedures, and safety approvals in place before any powered or flight testing.
  6. Testing & demonstration: subsystem and full-system testing, culminating in the end-to-end mission demonstration.
  7. Final review & symposium: final results, final report, and the project symposium.
